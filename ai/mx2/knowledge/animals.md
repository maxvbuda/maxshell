## Mammals
aka: what is a mammal
Mammals are warm-blooded animals that have hair or fur and feed their babies milk. Most give birth to live young — except the platypus and echidnas, which lay eggs. Humans, dogs, whales, bats and elephants are all mammals.

There are about 6,500 species of mammal. The blue whale is the largest; the tiniest is the bumblebee bat, which weighs about as much as a paperclip. Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly.
Q: Is a whale a mammal? | Yes — whales breathe air, are warm-blooded and feed their calves milk.
Q: Which mammals lay eggs? | The platypus and echidnas, known as monotremes.

## Birds
aka: what is a bird
Birds are warm-blooded animals with feathers, beaks and wings, and they lay hard-shelled eggs. Most can fly, thanks to light, hollow bones and powerful chest muscles, but some, like penguins, ostriches and emus, can’t. Birds are the living descendants of dinosaurs.

There are about 11,000 species. The ostrich is the largest bird, the bee hummingbird the smallest, and the peregrine falcon the fastest animal of all, diving at over 300 km/h. Many birds migrate thousands of kilometres each year.
Q: Are birds related to dinosaurs? | Yes — birds evolved from small feathered dinosaurs, so they’re living dinosaurs.
Q: What is the fastest bird? | The peregrine falcon, which can dive at over 300 km/h.

## Reptiles
aka: what is a reptile
Reptiles are cold-blooded animals with dry, scaly skin, and most lay eggs on land. They include snakes, lizards, turtles, tortoises, crocodiles and alligators. Being cold-blooded means they rely on their surroundings for heat, which is why they bask in the sun.

The saltwater crocodile is the largest living reptile. Some tortoises live for well over 150 years.

## Amphibians
aka: what is an amphibian, frogs
Amphibians — frogs, toads, salamanders and newts — usually start life in water and live on land as adults. A frog begins as an egg, hatches into a tadpole with gills and a tail, then grows legs and lungs and becomes a frog. This change is called metamorphosis.

Amphibians have thin, moist skin that they can breathe through, which makes them very sensitive to pollution.

## Fish
aka: what is a fish
Fish live in water, breathe with gills and usually have fins and scales. Most are cold-blooded. Their gills pull oxygen out of water as it flows over them. There are more than 34,000 species — more than all other vertebrates combined.

The whale shark is the largest fish (it’s a shark, not a whale). Sharks have skeletons made of cartilage instead of bone.
Q: How do fish breathe? | With gills, which take oxygen out of the water that flows over them.

## Insects
aka: what is an insect, bugs
Insects have six legs, three body parts (head, thorax and abdomen), and usually two antennae and wings. They are the most numerous animals on Earth, with over a million known species, including beetles, ants, bees, butterflies and flies.

Bees pollinate many of the crops we eat. Ants live in colonies that can hold millions of workers. Spiders are not insects — they have eight legs and belong to the arachnids.
Q: Is a spider an insect? | No. Spiders have eight legs and two body parts; they’re arachnids.
Q: How many legs does an insect have? | Six.

## Dogs
aka: dog, dogs
Dogs were the first animals people domesticated, at least 15,000 years ago, descending from ancient wolves. Today there are hundreds of breeds, from tiny Chihuahuas to huge Great Danes. Dogs have an incredible sense of smell — tens of thousands of times better than ours — which is why they’re used to find people and detect things.

Dogs are social pack animals, which is part of why they bond so closely with people. They usually live 10 to 15 years.
Q: How good is a dog's sense of smell? | Tens of thousands of times better than a human’s.

## Cats
aka: cat, cats
Cats have lived alongside people for about 10,000 years, first welcomed because they hunted the mice that raided grain stores. They are skilled hunters with sharp retractable claws, excellent night vision and whiskers that sense movement in the air.

Cats sleep 12 to 16 hours a day. They purr when they’re content, and sometimes to soothe themselves. A group of cats is called a clowder.

## Elephants
aka: elephant
Elephants are the largest land animals. There are African elephants (the biggest, with large ears shaped a bit like Africa) and Asian elephants. Their trunk is a long nose and upper lip combined, with tens of thousands of muscles — they use it to breathe, smell, drink, grab food and greet each other.

Elephants live in close family groups led by the oldest female, the matriarch. They are highly intelligent, have excellent memories and seem to mourn their dead.
Q: What is the largest land animal? | The African elephant.

## Whales
aka: whale, blue whale
Whales are huge marine mammals: they breathe air through a blowhole and feed their calves milk. The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived — up to 30 metres long and heavier than any dinosaur — yet it eats mostly tiny shrimp-like krill.

Some whales, like humpbacks, sing complex songs that travel for kilometres underwater. Many whales migrate huge distances between feeding and breeding grounds.
Q: What is the largest animal ever? | The blue whale, which can reach about 30 metres long.

## Sharks
aka: shark
Sharks have been around for over 400 million years — longer than trees. Their skeletons are made of cartilage, not bone, and many shed and replace thousands of teeth over their lives. They can sense tiny electrical signals from other animals.

There are over 500 species. Most are harmless to people; the largest, the whale shark, eats plankton. Many shark species are endangered because of overfishing.

## Dinosaurs
aka: dinosaur, dinosaurs, t rex
Dinosaurs were reptiles that dominated land for about 165 million years, until an asteroid impact 66 million years ago wiped most of them out. They ranged from chicken-sized hunters to giant long-necked plant-eaters like Argentinosaurus, over 30 metres long.

Tyrannosaurus rex was one of the largest meat-eaters, with teeth as long as bananas. We know about dinosaurs from fossils. Birds are their living descendants.
Q: When did the dinosaurs die out? | About 66 million years ago, after a huge asteroid struck Earth.
Q: Did people live with dinosaurs? | No — the last dinosaurs died about 66 million years before the first humans.

## Octopuses
aka: octopus
Octopuses are soft-bodied sea animals with eight arms, related to squid and snails. They have three hearts, blue blood, and a brain plus clusters of nerves in each arm — the arms can almost think for themselves.

They are very clever: they solve puzzles, open jars and escape from tanks. They can change colour and texture in a fraction of a second to hide, and squirt ink to escape predators.
Q: How many hearts does an octopus have? | Three.

## Bees
aka: bee, honeybee
Bees are flying insects that feed on nectar and pollen from flowers. As they move from flower to flower, they carry pollen, which lets plants make seeds and fruit — about a third of the food we eat depends on pollinators like bees.

Honeybees live in colonies of tens of thousands, with a single queen who lays the eggs, worker bees (all female) and drones. They tell each other where flowers are with a “waggle dance”, and turn nectar into honey to store for winter.

## Penguins
aka: penguin
Penguins are flightless seabirds that live almost entirely in the Southern Hemisphere. Their wings have become flippers, making them superb swimmers — some dive over 500 metres deep. Thick feathers and fat keep them warm.

Emperor penguins, the largest, breed in the Antarctic winter: the fathers balance the egg on their feet for two months without eating, huddling together against the cold.

## Endangered animals
aka: extinction, endangered species
An endangered species is one at risk of dying out. The main threats are habitat loss (forests and wetlands turned into farms or cities), hunting and poaching, pollution, climate change and invasive species. Tigers, orangutans, rhinos and many frogs are endangered.

Conservation helps: protected areas, anti-poaching work and breeding programmes have brought animals like the giant panda and humpback whale back from the brink.

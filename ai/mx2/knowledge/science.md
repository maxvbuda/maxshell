## Photosynthesis
aka: how plants make food, how plants get energy
Photosynthesis is how plants, algae and some bacteria make their own food. In their leaves, a green pigment called chlorophyll captures energy from sunlight. The plant uses that energy to combine water (taken up by its roots) and carbon dioxide (from the air) into glucose, a sugar it uses for energy and growth. Oxygen is released as a by-product.

The overall recipe is: carbon dioxide + water + light → glucose + oxygen. Almost all the oxygen we breathe and nearly all the food energy on Earth comes from photosynthesis, either directly from plants or from animals that eat them.
Q: What gas do plants give off during photosynthesis? | Oxygen. Plants take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen as a by-product of photosynthesis.
Q: Why are leaves green? | Because of chlorophyll, the pigment that captures sunlight. It absorbs red and blue light and reflects green, so leaves look green.
Q: What do plants need for photosynthesis? | Sunlight, water and carbon dioxide — plus chlorophyll in their leaves to capture the light.

## Gravity
aka: what gravity is, why things fall
Gravity is the force that pulls things with mass towards each other. The more mass something has, the stronger its pull, and the pull gets weaker with distance. Earth’s gravity keeps us on the ground, makes things fall, and holds the Moon in orbit. The Sun’s gravity keeps the planets orbiting around it.

Isaac Newton described gravity mathematically in 1687. In 1915 Albert Einstein explained it more deeply with general relativity: mass bends space and time, and objects follow those curves. Near Earth’s surface, falling objects speed up by about 9.8 metres per second every second, if you ignore air resistance.
Q: Who discovered gravity? | Isaac Newton described the law of gravity in 1687; Albert Einstein later explained it as the bending of space and time.
Q: Why don't we feel the Earth spinning? | Because we move along with it at a steady speed. You only feel changes in speed or direction, and the Earth’s spin is very smooth.
Q: Is there gravity in space? | Yes. Astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they are constantly falling around the Earth, not because gravity is missing.

## Atoms
aka: an atom, what things are made of
Atoms are the tiny building blocks of all matter. Each atom has a nucleus in the middle, made of protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge), surrounded by electrons (negative charge). The number of protons decides which element an atom is: one proton is hydrogen, six is carbon, eight is oxygen.

Atoms are unbelievably small — about ten million of them would fit across the full stop at the end of this sentence. Atoms join together to make molecules, like water (two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom).
Q: What are atoms made of? | A nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons moving around it.
Q: What decides which element an atom is? | The number of protons in its nucleus — its atomic number.

## The periodic table
aka: periodic table, chemical elements, elements
The periodic table lists all the known chemical elements — about 118 of them — in order of how many protons their atoms have. Elements in the same column behave in similar ways: the far-right column holds the noble gases like helium and neon, which hardly react with anything, while the far-left column holds very reactive metals like sodium.

Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the first periodic table in 1869 and even left gaps for elements that hadn’t been discovered yet. Most elements are metals; a few, like oxygen and nitrogen, are gases at room temperature, and two — mercury and bromine — are liquids.
Q: How many elements are there? | 118 are known so far.
Q: Who made the periodic table? | Dmitri Mendeleev, in 1869.
Q: What is the most common element in the universe? | Hydrogen — about three quarters of all normal matter.

## States of matter
aka: solid liquid and gas, solids liquids and gases
Matter usually comes in three states: solid, liquid and gas. In a solid, particles are packed tightly and hold their shape. In a liquid, they are close but can slide past each other, so liquids flow and take the shape of their container. In a gas, particles are far apart and move freely, filling any space.

Heating or cooling changes the state: ice melts into water at 0 °C, and water boils into steam at 100 °C (at sea level). A fourth state, plasma, is a super-hot gas with electrically charged particles — it’s what stars and lightning are made of.
Q: At what temperature does water boil? | 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level. It boils at a lower temperature high up on mountains.
Q: What is plasma? | A super-hot state of matter where atoms lose electrons, making it electrically charged. Stars and lightning are plasma.

## Energy
aka: what energy is, forms of energy
Energy is the ability to do work — to move things, heat them, or make them change. It comes in many forms: movement (kinetic energy), stored energy (potential energy, like a stretched rubber band or a raised ball), heat, light, sound, electrical and chemical energy (stored in food and fuel).

Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes form. A car turns chemical energy in fuel into movement and heat; a solar panel turns light into electricity. Energy is measured in joules; food energy is often measured in calories.
Q: Can energy be created? | No. Energy can only change from one form to another — this is called the conservation of energy.

## Electricity
aka: how electricity works, electric current
Electricity is the flow of tiny charged particles called electrons through a material, like the copper in a wire. For current to flow, it needs a complete loop — a circuit — and something pushing it, like a battery or a power station. The push is measured in volts, the flow in amps, and how much a material resists the flow in ohms.

Metals conduct electricity well; rubber and plastic don’t, which is why wires are wrapped in plastic. Most of our electricity is generated by spinning magnets inside coils of wire — turned by steam, wind or falling water.
Q: What is a circuit? | A complete loop that electricity can flow around, from a power source, through things like bulbs, and back again.
Q: Why are wires covered in plastic? | Plastic doesn’t conduct electricity, so it keeps the current in the wire and stops shocks.

## Magnets
aka: magnetism, how magnets work
Magnets have two ends, a north pole and a south pole. Opposite poles attract and like poles repel. Magnets pull on some metals — mainly iron, nickel and cobalt — but not on others like aluminium or copper.

The Earth itself is a giant magnet, because of the molten iron moving in its core. That’s why a compass needle points north. Electricity and magnetism are closely linked: a current in a coil of wire makes a magnet (an electromagnet), and moving a magnet near a wire makes a current.
Q: Why does a compass point north? | Its needle is a small magnet that lines up with Earth’s magnetic field.

## Light
aka: what light is, how light travels
Light is a form of energy that travels as waves, extremely fast — about 300,000 kilometres per second, the fastest anything can go. It travels in straight lines, bounces off surfaces (reflection) and bends when it passes from one material into another (refraction), which is why a straw looks bent in a glass of water.

White light is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow. A prism or raindrops split it into red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. Visible light is only a small part of a bigger family that includes radio waves, microwaves, X-rays and ultraviolet.
Q: How fast is light? | About 300,000 km per second — light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
Q: How do rainbows form? | Sunlight enters raindrops, bends and splits into its colours, and reflects back towards you.

## Sound
aka: how sound works, sound waves
Sound is a vibration that travels through a material — air, water or solids — as a wave. When you speak, your vocal cords vibrate, pushing air molecules, which push the next ones, until the vibration reaches someone’s ear. Sound can’t travel through empty space, so space is silent.

How fast something vibrates decides the pitch: fast vibrations sound high, slow ones low. The size of the vibration decides how loud it is, measured in decibels. Sound travels about 343 metres per second in air — much slower than light, which is why you see lightning before you hear thunder.
Q: Can sound travel in space? | No. Sound needs a material to travel through, and space is almost empty.
Q: Why do you see lightning before hearing thunder? | Light travels almost a million times faster than sound.

## Cells
aka: a cell, what living things are made of
Cells are the smallest living units — every living thing is made of one or more of them. Bacteria are a single cell; your body has around 30 trillion. Most cells have a membrane that controls what goes in and out, a jelly-like cytoplasm where work gets done, and genetic instructions (DNA).

Animal and plant cells keep their DNA in a nucleus. Plant cells also have a stiff cell wall and chloroplasts for photosynthesis. Different cells do different jobs: nerve cells carry signals, red blood cells carry oxygen, and muscle cells contract to move you.
Q: How many cells are in the human body? | Roughly 30 trillion, plus a similar number of bacteria living in and on us.
Q: What is the difference between plant and animal cells? | Plant cells have a cell wall and chloroplasts for photosynthesis; animal cells don’t.

## DNA
aka: genes, what dna is
DNA is the molecule that carries the instructions for building and running a living thing. It is shaped like a twisted ladder — a double helix. The rungs are pairs of four chemical “letters” (A, T, C and G), and their order spells out the instructions, the way letters spell words.

A gene is a section of DNA with the instructions for one thing, usually a protein. You inherit half your DNA from each parent, which is why you share traits with your family. Almost every cell in your body contains a full copy — about 3 billion letters long.
Q: What shape is DNA? | A double helix — like a ladder twisted into a spiral.
Q: Who discovered the structure of DNA? | James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin.

## Evolution
aka: natural selection, how species change
Evolution is how living things change over many generations. Individuals in a species vary a little, and some of those differences help them survive and have more offspring — so those traits become more common over time. This process, natural selection, was described by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in the 1850s.

Over millions of years, small changes add up to new species. Fossils, DNA comparisons and observations of living things all support evolution. It explains why all life shares common features, and why animals are so well suited to where they live.
Q: Who came up with the theory of evolution? | Charles Darwin, with Alfred Russel Wallace, in the 1850s; Darwin published “On the Origin of Species” in 1859.

## Chemical reactions
aka: chemical reaction, chemistry
A chemical reaction is when substances change into new substances by rearranging their atoms. Burning wood, rusting iron, baking a cake and digesting food are all chemical reactions. Signs of a reaction include a change of colour, bubbles of gas, light or heat, or a new solid forming.

Atoms are never lost in a reaction — they just swap partners. That’s why chemists balance equations: the same atoms appear on both sides. Some reactions release energy (like burning), and some absorb it (like a cold pack).
Q: Is melting ice a chemical reaction? | No, it’s a physical change: water is still water. A chemical reaction makes new substances.

## Acids and bases
aka: ph, acid, alkali
Acids taste sour and bases (alkalis) feel slippery; both can be dangerous when strong. The pH scale measures how acidic or basic something is, from 0 to 14: 7 is neutral (pure water), below 7 is acidic (lemon juice is about 2), and above 7 is basic (soap is about 10).

When an acid and a base react, they neutralise each other and make water and a salt. Your stomach uses strong acid to digest food; antacid tablets are mild bases that neutralise it.
Q: What is the pH of water? | 7, which is neutral.
Q: Is lemon juice an acid? | Yes — its pH is about 2, because of citric acid.

## Newton's laws of motion
aka: laws of motion, newton's laws, force and motion
Isaac Newton’s three laws describe how things move. First: an object stays still, or keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. Second: force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma), so heavier things need more force to speed up. Third: every action has an equal and opposite reaction — push on a wall and it pushes back on you.

These laws explain why seat belts matter (your body keeps moving when the car stops), why rockets work (they push gas down, and the gas pushes them up), and how engineers design everything from bridges to rollercoasters.
Q: What is Newton's third law? | For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
Q: What does F = ma mean? | Force equals mass times acceleration — the bigger the mass, the more force it takes to change its speed.

## Friction
aka: what friction is
Friction is the force that resists things sliding past each other. Rough surfaces have more friction than smooth ones. Friction lets us walk without slipping, lets car tyres grip the road, and lets brakes slow things down — but it also wears things out and wastes energy as heat.

That’s why you can warm your hands by rubbing them together, and why we use oil to reduce friction in engines. Ice has very little friction, which is why it’s slippery.

## Temperature and heat
aka: heat, temperature
Temperature measures how hot or cold something is — really, how fast its particles are moving. Heat is energy that flows from hotter things to colder ones, until they’re the same temperature. It moves in three ways: conduction (through touching), convection (through moving liquids and gases, like warm air rising) and radiation (like sunlight).

Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius, Fahrenheit or Kelvin. Water freezes at 0 °C (32 °F) and boils at 100 °C (212 °F). Absolute zero, −273.15 °C, is the coldest anything can be.
Q: What is absolute zero? | −273.15 °C (0 kelvin), the coldest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible movement.
Q: How do you convert Celsius to Fahrenheit? | Multiply by 9, divide by 5, then add 32. For example, 20 °C is 68 °F.

## The water cycle
aka: water cycle, how rain forms
The water cycle is how water moves around the Earth. The Sun heats oceans, lakes and rivers, and water evaporates into invisible water vapour. As the vapour rises it cools and condenses into tiny droplets, forming clouds. When the droplets join into bigger drops, they fall as rain, snow or hail (precipitation).

The water flows into rivers and underground, back to the sea, and the cycle starts again. It’s the same water that has been cycling for billions of years — the water you drink may once have been drunk by a dinosaur.
Q: How do clouds form? | Water vapour rises, cools and condenses into tiny water droplets or ice crystals, which we see as clouds.

## Vaccines
aka: how vaccines work, vaccination
A vaccine trains your immune system to recognise a germ without you having to get sick. It contains a harmless piece or weakened form of the germ, or instructions for your cells to make one harmless part of it. Your immune system responds by making antibodies and memory cells.

If the real germ ever arrives, your body recognises it and fights it off quickly. Vaccines have wiped out smallpox and nearly eliminated polio. When enough people are vaccinated, a disease struggles to spread — protecting people who can’t be vaccinated too.
Q: Who invented the first vaccine? | Edward Jenner, in 1796, for smallpox.

## Climate change
aka: global warming, climate crisis
Climate change is the long-term warming of the Earth’s climate, mainly caused by people burning coal, oil and gas. Burning them releases carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases, which trap heat in the atmosphere like a blanket. Since the 1800s, the planet has warmed by about 1.2 °C.

The effects include hotter heatwaves, melting ice and rising seas, and changes to rainfall and storms. Ways to slow it include switching to clean energy like solar and wind, using energy more efficiently, protecting forests and changing how we travel and farm.
Q: What causes climate change? | Mainly greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide from burning coal, oil and gas, which trap heat in the atmosphere.
Q: What is the greenhouse effect? | Gases in the atmosphere trap some of the Sun’s heat, keeping Earth warm. Adding more of them traps more heat.

## Volcanoes
aka: volcano, how volcanoes work
A volcano is an opening in the Earth’s crust where molten rock, gas and ash escape. Deep underground, rock melts into magma; because it’s lighter than the rock around it, it rises. When pressure builds up, it erupts through the surface as lava.

Most volcanoes sit where the Earth’s tectonic plates meet, like the “Ring of Fire” around the Pacific Ocean. Eruptions can be gentle lava flows, like in Hawaii, or huge explosions. Volcanic soil is very fertile, which is why people often farm near volcanoes.
Q: What is the difference between magma and lava? | Magma is molten rock underground; once it reaches the surface, it’s called lava.

## Earthquakes
aka: earthquake, tectonic plates
The Earth’s outer shell is broken into huge pieces called tectonic plates that slowly move — a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Where plates grind past each other, stress builds up until the rock suddenly slips. That sudden release shakes the ground: an earthquake.

Earthquakes are measured by their magnitude; each step up the scale means about 32 times more energy. Under the ocean, earthquakes can cause tsunamis — giant waves. Buildings in earthquake zones are designed to sway instead of breaking.
Q: What causes earthquakes? | Tectonic plates getting stuck and then suddenly slipping, releasing energy that shakes the ground.

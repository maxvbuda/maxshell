## The Sun
aka: the sun, our star
The Sun is a star — a huge ball of hot plasma, mostly hydrogen and helium, at the centre of our solar system. In its core, hydrogen atoms fuse into helium, releasing enormous energy as light and heat. The surface is about 5,500 °C and the core about 15 million °C.

The Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth and holds 99.8% of all the mass in the solar system. It’s about 4.6 billion years old, roughly halfway through its life. Its light takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach us.
Q: How hot is the Sun? | About 5,500 °C on the surface and around 15 million °C in the core.
Q: How long does sunlight take to reach Earth? | About 8 minutes and 20 seconds.
Q: Is the Sun a star? | Yes — a fairly ordinary, medium-sized star.

## The solar system
aka: solar system, planets
The solar system is the Sun and everything that orbits it: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets like Pluto, and countless asteroids and comets. In order from the Sun, the planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

The four inner planets are small and rocky. The four outer ones are giants: Jupiter and Saturn are mostly gas, and Uranus and Neptune are “ice giants”. Between Mars and Jupiter lies the asteroid belt. A handy memory trick for the order is “My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming”.
Q: How many planets are there? | Eight: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
Q: What is the biggest planet? | Jupiter — more than 1,300 Earths would fit inside it.
Q: What is the smallest planet? | Mercury.
Q: What is the hottest planet? | Venus, at about 465 °C — its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat, making it hotter than Mercury.

## The Moon
aka: the moon, earth's moon
The Moon is Earth’s only natural satellite. It orbits us about once every 27 days, about 384,000 km away, and is about a quarter of Earth’s width. It doesn’t make its own light — it shines by reflecting sunlight, and its phases (new, crescent, half, full) come from how much of its sunlit side we can see.

The Moon’s gravity causes the ocean tides. It always shows us the same face, because it spins exactly once per orbit. In 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin of Apollo 11 became the first people to walk on it; twelve astronauts have walked there in total.
Q: Who was the first person on the Moon? | Neil Armstrong, on 20 July 1969, during the Apollo 11 mission.
Q: Why does the Moon have phases? | As it orbits Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half.
Q: What causes tides? | Mainly the Moon’s gravity pulling on the oceans, with some help from the Sun.

## Mars
aka: mars, the red planet
Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun. It’s red because its surface is covered in iron oxide — rust. It’s about half Earth’s size, with a thin atmosphere of carbon dioxide, and it’s very cold: on average about −60 °C.

Mars has the tallest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons, three times the height of Everest, and two small moons, Phobos and Deimos. Rovers like Curiosity and Perseverance have found that it once had rivers and lakes. A day on Mars is 24 hours and 37 minutes.
Q: Why is Mars red? | Its soil and rocks contain iron oxide — rust.
Q: Is there water on Mars? | Yes, as ice at the poles and underground; long ago it had liquid water on the surface.

## Jupiter
aka: jupiter
Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and the biggest by far — more massive than all the other planets combined. It’s a gas giant made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface to stand on. Its colourful bands are clouds whipped by strong winds.

The Great Red Spot is a storm larger than Earth that has been raging for hundreds of years. Jupiter has at least 95 moons, including Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system, and Europa, which may hide an ocean under its ice. Jupiter spins so fast that a day there lasts under 10 hours.
Q: What is the Great Red Spot? | A giant storm on Jupiter, bigger than Earth, that has lasted for centuries.

## Saturn
aka: saturn
Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun, a gas giant famous for its bright rings. The rings are made of billions of pieces of ice and rock, from dust-sized to house-sized, and are hundreds of thousands of kilometres wide but mostly only tens of metres thick.

Saturn is so light for its size that it would float in a big enough bathtub. It has well over 100 moons; the largest, Titan, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane.
Q: What are Saturn's rings made of? | Countless chunks of ice and rock, from tiny grains to boulders.

## Stars
aka: a star, how stars work, star life cycle
Stars are giant balls of hot gas that make their own light by nuclear fusion in their cores. They form inside clouds of gas and dust called nebulae, when gravity pulls the gas together until it’s hot and dense enough to fuse hydrogen.

A star’s mass decides its life. Medium stars like the Sun swell into red giants and end as white dwarfs. Much bigger stars burn out faster and explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes. Star colour shows temperature: blue stars are the hottest, red the coolest. Apart from the Sun, the nearest star is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light-years away.
Q: What is the closest star to Earth? | The Sun. After that, Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light-years away.
Q: Why do stars twinkle? | Their light is bent this way and that by moving air in our atmosphere.

## Black holes
aka: black hole
A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing — not even light — can escape. They form when a very massive star collapses at the end of its life, squeezing its mass into a tiny space. The boundary you can’t come back from is called the event horizon.

Supermassive black holes, millions or billions of times the Sun’s mass, sit at the centres of most galaxies, including our own Milky Way. In 2019 astronomers took the first image of a black hole’s shadow, in the galaxy M87.
Q: Can light escape a black hole? | No — once past the event horizon, not even light can get out.

## Galaxies
aka: galaxy, the milky way
A galaxy is a huge collection of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is a spiral with a few hundred billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across. The Sun sits in one of its spiral arms, about two thirds of the way out.

There are probably around two trillion galaxies in the observable universe. The nearest large one, Andromeda, is 2.5 million light-years away and is heading towards us — it will merge with the Milky Way in about 4.5 billion years.
Q: What galaxy do we live in? | The Milky Way, a spiral galaxy.
Q: How many stars are in the Milky Way? | Roughly 100 to 400 billion.

## Light-years
aka: light year, what is a light year
A light-year is a distance, not a time: it’s how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres. Space is so big that ordinary units become unhelpful, so astronomers use light-years.

When you look at a star 100 light-years away, you see light that left it 100 years ago — you’re looking into the past. The farthest galaxies we can see are over 13 billion light-years away.
Q: Is a light-year a unit of time? | No, it’s a distance — how far light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion km.

## The Big Bang
aka: big bang, how the universe began
The Big Bang is the scientific explanation of how the universe began, about 13.8 billion years ago. Everything was once packed into an incredibly hot, dense state, which then expanded and cooled. It wasn’t an explosion in space — space itself started expanding, and still is today.

As it cooled, the first atoms formed, then stars and galaxies. Evidence includes the faint glow left over from the early universe (the cosmic microwave background) and the fact that distant galaxies are moving away from us.
Q: How old is the universe? | About 13.8 billion years.

## Comets and asteroids
aka: comet, asteroid, meteor, meteorite
Asteroids are rocky or metal bodies orbiting the Sun, most of them in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Comets are icy bodies from the outer solar system; when one comes close to the Sun, its ice turns to gas, making a glowing head and a long tail that points away from the Sun.

A meteor is a small piece of space rock burning up in our atmosphere — a “shooting star”. If a piece survives and lands, it’s a meteorite. An asteroid impact 66 million years ago helped wipe out the dinosaurs.
Q: What is a shooting star? | A meteor — a small piece of space rock burning up as it enters Earth’s atmosphere.

## Astronauts and the ISS
aka: international space station, iss, astronaut
The International Space Station (ISS) is a large spacecraft that orbits Earth about 400 km up, circling the planet every 90 minutes — so its crew sees about 16 sunrises a day. It has been continuously lived on since 2000, built and run by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada.

Astronauts on board float because they’re in constant free fall. They exercise for about two hours a day to keep their muscles and bones strong, sleep in sleeping bags tied to the wall, and do experiments that can only be done in space.
Q: How fast does the ISS travel? | About 28,000 km per hour, circling Earth every 90 minutes.
Q: Who was the first person in space? | Yuri Gagarin of the Soviet Union, on 12 April 1961.

## Seasons
aka: why we have seasons, why is it hot in summer
Earth has seasons because it is tilted by about 23.5 degrees. As it orbits the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans towards the Sun. The half tilted towards the Sun gets more direct sunlight and longer days — that’s summer — while the other half has winter.

It’s not about distance from the Sun: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during the northern winter. Near the equator, the seasons hardly change; near the poles, summer brings days when the Sun never sets.
Q: Why do we have seasons? | Because Earth is tilted, so each hemisphere takes turns leaning towards the Sun during the year.

## Day and night
aka: why we have day and night
We have day and night because the Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours. The side facing the Sun has daytime; the side facing away has night. The Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west because the Earth spins from west to east.

The Earth’s spin is slowly getting slower — days were only about 22 hours long when dinosaurs lived.
Q: How long does Earth take to spin once? | About 24 hours (23 hours 56 minutes relative to the stars).
Q: How long does Earth take to go around the Sun? | About 365.25 days — the extra quarter day is why we have leap years.

## The heart
aka: heart, how the heart works
The heart is a muscular pump about the size of your fist. It beats around 100,000 times a day, pushing blood through your blood vessels to deliver oxygen and nutrients to every cell. It has four chambers: the right side pumps blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen, and the left side pumps that oxygen-rich blood to the rest of the body.

A resting adult heart beats about 60 to 100 times a minute. Exercise, a healthy diet and not smoking keep it strong.
Q: How many chambers does the heart have? | Four: two atria on top and two ventricles below.
Q: How many times does the heart beat a day? | About 100,000 times.

## Blood
aka: what blood does, blood cells
Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste around the body. An adult has about 5 litres of it. It’s made of plasma (a yellowish liquid), red blood cells that carry oxygen, white blood cells that fight infection, and platelets that help it clot when you’re cut.

Red blood cells get their colour from haemoglobin, an iron-rich protein that grabs oxygen. People have different blood types — A, B, AB and O, each positive or negative — which matters for blood transfusions.
Q: Why is blood red? | Because of haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein in red blood cells.
Q: How much blood is in the human body? | About 5 litres in an adult.

## The brain
aka: brain, how the brain works
The brain is the body’s control centre. It weighs about 1.4 kg and contains around 86 billion nerve cells (neurons), which send electrical and chemical signals to each other. It controls your thoughts, memories, senses, movements and emotions, as well as automatic things like breathing and your heartbeat.

Different parts do different jobs: the cerebrum handles thinking and senses, the cerebellum balance and coordination, and the brainstem keeps you alive by running breathing and heart rate. Sleep is when the brain sorts and stores memories.
Q: How many neurons are in the brain? | About 86 billion.
Q: What part of the brain controls balance? | The cerebellum.

## Lungs and breathing
aka: lungs, how breathing works
When you breathe in, a dome-shaped muscle called the diaphragm pulls down and air rushes into your lungs. Inside, air travels through smaller and smaller tubes into millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli. There, oxygen passes into your blood and carbon dioxide passes out, to be breathed away.

An adult breathes about 12 to 20 times a minute at rest — over 20,000 times a day. Your right lung is slightly bigger than your left, which makes room for the heart.
Q: What gas do we breathe out? | Carbon dioxide (plus the nitrogen and leftover oxygen from the air).

## Digestion
aka: digestive system, how digestion works
Digestion breaks food into small molecules your body can absorb. It starts in the mouth, where teeth chew and saliva begins breaking down starch. Food then travels down the oesophagus to the stomach, where acid and enzymes churn it into a thick liquid.

In the small intestine — about 6 metres long — nutrients are absorbed into the blood. The large intestine absorbs water, and whatever is left leaves the body as waste. The whole trip takes about one to three days.
Q: How long is the small intestine? | About 6 metres in an adult.

## Bones and the skeleton
aka: skeleton, bones
The adult human skeleton has 206 bones. It holds you up, protects soft organs (your skull protects your brain and your ribs your heart and lungs), and works with muscles to move you. Bones are living tissue: they grow, repair themselves when broken, and make blood cells in their marrow.

Babies are born with around 300 bones, many of which fuse together as they grow. The biggest bone is the thigh bone (femur); the smallest is the stirrup bone in the ear. Calcium and exercise keep bones strong.
Q: How many bones are in the human body? | 206 in an adult. Babies have about 300, which fuse as they grow.
Q: What is the largest bone in the body? | The femur, or thigh bone.

## Muscles
aka: how muscles work
Muscles move your body by contracting — getting shorter — and pulling on bones. Because they can only pull, they work in pairs: your biceps bend your arm and your triceps straighten it. You have over 600 muscles.

There are three types: skeletal muscles that you control, smooth muscles in organs like the stomach that work automatically, and heart muscle, which never tires. Exercise makes muscles stronger by causing tiny tears that heal thicker.

## The immune system
aka: immune system, how the body fights germs
The immune system defends the body against germs like bacteria and viruses. Skin and mucus are the first barriers. If germs get in, white blood cells attack them; some swallow invaders, while others make antibodies that stick to a specific germ and mark it for destruction.

The immune system remembers germs it has beaten before, so the next infection is fought off faster — that’s how vaccines work. A fever is part of the fight: higher body temperature makes it harder for germs to multiply.
Q: What are antibodies? | Proteins made by white blood cells that latch on to a specific germ so it can be destroyed.

## The five senses
aka: senses, sight hearing smell taste touch
The five classic senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. Eyes turn light into signals; ears turn vibrations into signals; the nose and tongue detect chemicals; and skin senses pressure, heat, cold and pain. The brain combines them all into your picture of the world.

We actually have more than five: balance, the sense of where your body parts are (proprioception), and sensing hunger or temperature inside the body. Most of what we call “taste” is really smell — which is why food tastes bland with a blocked nose.
Q: What are the five senses? | Sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch.

## Sleep
aka: why we sleep, how much sleep do i need
Sleep lets your body and brain rest and repair. While you sleep, your brain sorts and stores memories, your body releases growth hormone and repairs tissue, and your immune system gets stronger. Sleep comes in cycles of light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep, when most dreaming happens.

Adults need about 7 to 9 hours a night, teenagers 8 to 10, and young children even more. Too little sleep makes it harder to concentrate, learn and stay in a good mood.
Q: How much sleep do teenagers need? | About 8 to 10 hours a night.
Q: When do we dream? | Mostly during REM (rapid eye movement) sleep.

## Nutrition
aka: healthy eating, nutrients, a balanced diet
Food gives your body energy and the building blocks it needs. The main nutrients are carbohydrates (energy), proteins (building and repairing), fats (energy and cell health), vitamins and minerals (many jobs, in small amounts), fibre (digestion) and water.

A balanced diet includes plenty of vegetables and fruit, whole grains, protein such as beans, fish, eggs or meat, and some dairy or alternatives, while keeping sugary drinks and very processed foods low. Drinking water throughout the day matters too.
Q: What do proteins do? | They build and repair your body — muscles, skin, enzymes and more.
Q: What is vitamin C good for? | It helps heal wounds and supports the immune system; citrus fruits, peppers and strawberries are good sources.

## Teeth
aka: teeth, how many teeth
Adults have 32 permanent teeth; children have 20 baby teeth, which fall out between about age 6 and 12. There are incisors for cutting, canines for tearing, and premolars and molars for grinding. Tooth enamel, the hard outer layer, is the hardest substance in the human body.

Bacteria in your mouth turn sugar into acid, which causes cavities. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, flossing and cutting down on sugary snacks keep teeth healthy.
Q: How many teeth do adults have? | 32, including the four wisdom teeth.

## Skin
aka: the skin
Skin is the body’s largest organ, covering about 2 square metres in an adult. It keeps germs out and water in, helps control temperature (by sweating and changing blood flow) and senses touch, heat and pain. The top layer constantly sheds dead cells — you lose millions a day.

Melanin, a pigment in skin, gives it colour and protects it from the Sun’s ultraviolet rays. Sunscreen and shade help prevent sunburn and skin cancer.
Q: What is the largest organ in the body? | The skin.
